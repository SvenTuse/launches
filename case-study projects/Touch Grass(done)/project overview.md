## 1. Narrative, meta, technology and product

### A meme with a demonstrable action

Touch Grass turns an internet instruction into a product loop: stop watching a chart, walk to a real company location and collect a stock token. Familiar brands serve as both collectible icons and destinations. “Pokémon GO for stocks” works because it explains map discovery, physical movement and rewards in one recognizable comparison. The RWA/tokenization theme gives the meme topical relevance; the outdoor activity gives it something users can film. This framing appears in the supplied launch/demo posts and Sep 3 contract mentions.

The initial offer is stock-token fragments at company stores or offices, with rare full-token events. The expansion narrative is broader proof of presence: sponsored coins, café rewards, venue visits, receipt collectibles and reviews. It gives developers successive update topics rather than requiring a new story every week. The Sep 22 poll placed retail/restaurants and live sports/events ahead of exploration and tourism; the Sep 23 mall example then turned community feedback into a proposed product use case.

### Technology supporting the story

The published claim system pairs a location-checking backend with signed vouchers and a funded stock-token vault. Social sign-in and embedded wallets reduce the initial crypto setup burden. Anti-fraud checks, payout limits, wallet history, native mobile sensors and visible reserve/payout pages address abuse and credibility. The existence of a public interface is directly observed; detailed enforcement and security properties remain attributed to the project.

The game token and reward tokens serve different roles. GRASS is used in published holding requirements, fee allocation, buybacks and shop purchases. Loot represents other assets or sponsor-funded goods. Holding gates can connect token demand to a product action, but do not grant GRASS holders company equity or a proven share of profit.

Stock-token exposure is also different from owning the underlying company. Current issuer documentation describes chain tokens without legal or beneficial rights against the underlying issuer, and restricts US distribution. The project's equity language and US-access promotion therefore need correction before being reused. See the primary-source comparison in [other-info.md](other-info.md).

### Delivered product versus expanding claims

A visitor can inspect map clusters, city listings, payout tables, a public leaderboard, shop listings and a sponsorship form. Android is advertised by direct APK; iOS is still pending. Authenticated catches, payments and feature deployment were not tested.

The live map displayed 164 cities/864 points, while static copy described 163/862. The proof page displayed 53,591 transfers to 34,562 wallets, which establishes a rendered activity claim, not 34,562 human players. Marketing says receipt/review features are live, whereas docs/roadmap describe disabled or undeployed versions. Similar conflicts affect sponsor rails and reward rules. The product is a useful case, but its evidence should not be flattened into “everything works worldwide.”

## 2. How it attracted attention, why it may have worked, and how money could flow

### The visible attention mechanism

1. **Immediate comprehension.** The launch combines a couch-to-map video, a short stock/walk instruction and the CA. It has 228K displayed views and 1,058 interactions. The video still connects the user's existing behavior—looking at a phone—to the next action.
2. **Recognizable brands and consistent objects.** Apple, Tesla, NVIDIA and other cubes make the rewards concrete. Glossy lime letters, the TG avatar and the sky background match the website. A city or milestone can become a fresh creative without changing the visual language.
3. **Geography as distribution.** The 13-city post has 133K views; US access has 144K. Miami and Asia posts create local relevance. City requests invite replies, while user-submitted locations let the audience contribute to expansion.
4. **A public economic story.** The initial fee-split post reaches 123K; the revised 75%-to-protocol post reaches 54K. Payout and burn references provide a checkable destination for curiosity. This makes token mechanics part of the content calendar.
5. **Scarcity and competition.** One-place/one-window rewards, city codes, leaderboards and weekly prizes give users a reason to return. The legendary launch reaches 89K; the leaderboard prize post offers a concrete $150/$100/$50 schedule.
6. **User videos as distribution.** A Times Square demonstration makes the mechanic tangible. The 3x catch-video incentive reaches 108K and explicitly asks users to tag the account and ticker. It connects reward expenditure to content creation, although the dataset does not show how many videos or retained users it generated.
7. **Founder and social proof.** Founder articles and the quoted Wizard of Soho discussion add a human/product thesis. The supplied smart-follower count suggests the account entered crypto attention networks, but the extension's classification is not a verified endorsement.
8. **Repeated shipping and repair posts.** Android, social sign-in, Asia, sponsors, leaderboards and payout changes provide updates; bot bans and rejected-catch fixes show ongoing operational work. The cadence supports an active-team impression while also documenting genuine friction.

These are plausible contributors, not established causal effects. September 3–4 make up about 48.5% of the captured accumulated views. Older posts have had more time to accrue views. No paid-media ledger, follower attribution, onboarding conversion or cohort retention data is supplied, so the research cannot prove which creative caused adoption or price appreciation.

### Signals that should not be inflated

The five account posts above 100K views have relatively low visible engagement rates, so large reach does not automatically mean a committed audience. The 132-vote expansion poll is useful qualitative input, not market sizing. A current wallet count is not a user count, especially when the account reports throwaway-wallet farms.

Most main contract mentions—22/28—are copied vote solicitations. They are excluded from substantive positive discussion. The remaining six main posts are supportive but partly speculative and promotional; author compensation is unknown. The Sep 9 FUD response and later anti-fraud posts show that attention coexisted with drawdowns, abuse and dissatisfaction.

Generic Binance grass imagery and Robinhood slogans/tokenization news were quoted by the project. They are thematic association, not evidence that those companies endorsed, listed or invested in GRASS. Named collaborators in a post are also weaker evidence than a confirmed bilateral announcement.

### The economic model and its limits

The supplied history shows an initial 25% burn / 25% stock treasury / 50% team split of fees, revised to 37.5% / 37.5% / 25%. The mechanism can turn trading-fee income into visible reward funding and supply reduction while retaining a team share. **The trading fee rate is not supplied.** Multiplying $48.52M by a split percentage would invent revenue.

The current shop offers dollar-priced convenience products paid in GRASS plus ETH, with a published retained share and burn share. Sponsored venues fund rewards and may produce GRASS holding demand. Sponsor deposits chiefly buy rewards; the page says stock funding has no markup, so depositing $1,000 is not evidence the platform earned $1,000. Receipt/service monetization appears prospective or inconsistently deployed.

The possible loop is:
**attention → token trading/venue funding → fee receipts or funded rewards → outdoor catches → visible user content and proof → more attention.**
It is an inference from the described mechanisms, not a measured growth model. It can weaken when trading fades, bots absorb rewards, users face too much friction, or sponsors do not see profitable incremental visits.

The material supports a case of **substantial captured attention and an inspectable product**, alongside supplied ATH/volume claims. It does not prove profitable operations, realized founder proceeds or repeatable investment returns. To establish those outcomes, obtain actual fee/shop receipts, cost and refund records, human cohorts and retained sponsor revenue.

## 3. What a launch team can take from the case

### Replicable practices

- **Make the pitch a verb and a reward.** A user should understand the first action immediately and be able to demonstrate it in a short clip. Test comprehension with people outside crypto before attaching a token.
- **Launch a usable proof of concept with the CA.** A real map and a filmed catch support the narrative. Publish one canonical identity across the site, X and explorer; external articles in this case mixed founders and token contracts.
- **Give creatives one job each.** Use one consistent object language over recognizable local scenes. Publish a city, a reward, a user action or a number per creative. Pair stylized art with actual product recordings.
- **Plan a sequence of real releases.** Geographic additions, native distribution, easy sign-in, sponsored venues and competition create successive stories. Every post should link to the feature it claims is available.
- **Reward useful user evidence within a budget.** Demonstrated catches can advertise the product. Define validation, capped rewards and success measures; views alone do not justify an unlimited content subsidy.
- **Make trust inspectable.** Offer transaction links and distinguish payouts, wallets, verified humans and active users. Show a funded reward before somebody spends time walking to it.
- **Let venues and users create supply.** Suggested locations and sponsor-funded drops can extend the map beyond the team. Venue tools should be simple enough for a shopkeeper to use at the counter.
- **Use holding requirements only where the product needs them.** A sponsor or rare event gate creates a reason to keep the token, but must remain understandable. Do not confuse a holding gate with revenue.
- **Keep one published source for current rules.** This case's changing radii, caps, shop effects and feature flags create avoidable confusion. Mark historical posts with release notes or link to a maintained status page.
- **Treat anti-abuse, support and identity as launch work.** Wallet counts are easy to inflate. Design for repeat devices, retries, false refusals and human appeals before expanding reward expenditure.

### A practical pilot based on the evidence

Start with one walkable area and a small set of venues you can verify yourself. Secure funded rewards, build sign-in plus a map and one presence-verification path, then film a complete real visit and reward redemption. Publish the identity, current rules and reserve/payout evidence alongside the launch creative.

Run a short local content campaign: explanation/demo, venue reveal, rare event, actual user catch, payout proof and a concrete update. Invite genuine user clips and place suggestions. Introduce a paid venue pilot only after you can distinguish real incremental visits from reward farming.

Measure **verified human visitors, completed redemptions, cost per incremental venue visit, seven-day repeat participation, sponsor renewal, content cost per retained user, rejection/appeal rate and net receipts after rewards/support**. Set a fixed funded reward ceiling. Expand only when those figures support it. If using financial assets, resolve the issuer's eligibility/distribution rules first; the screenshot's US-success story is not sufficient authority.

Avoid copying fake votes, implied major-brand partnerships, unresolved equity claims or an assumption that token volume equals income. The transferable lesson is a demonstrable activity with coherent branding, funded incentives and credible evidence.

## 4. Ten similar ideas and how to implement/promote them

These are new concepts inspired by the case, not existing projects or verified demand forecasts. Prefer venue-funded goods or clearly eligible token rewards; a project token is optional until it serves a concrete function.

### 1. Coffee Quest

Build a map of participating independent cafés where a counter code proves a visit and unlocks a drink credit or collectible stamp. Launch on X with a one-take walk-and-redemption video and a weekly neighborhood route. Use a recognizable 3D coffee-bean mascot above real shop photographs, supported by genuine customer clips. Charge cafés a disclosed campaign/service fee and measure repeat customers rather than subsidizing unlimited free coffee.

### 2. Mall Circuit

Let a mall fund a four-stop route, with each store validating a short code and the completed route unlocking a fixed reward. Promote a timed weekend challenge on X with store partners reposting their own checkpoint. Creatives can show a simple four-node route, one inflatable numbered marker per storefront, and an actual completion screen. Monetize the campaign setup and reporting, with a fixed reward budget and unique-visitor deduplication.

### 3. Trail Treasure

Place funded collectibles or outdoor-store vouchers at safe, maintained trail checkpoints, with GPS and a trail steward's rotating code. Promote weekly routes and walker-made clips on X instead of promising income per mile. Use a consistent trail-compass object, real terrain photos and a simple before/after route card. Earn from outdoor-brand campaigns while capping rewards and avoiding inaccessible or unsafe locations.

### 4. Museum Passport

Create an on-chain or ordinary digital passport with collectible stamps issued only after a museum validates a visit. Release one exhibit/story per X post and invite visitors to show what they learned rather than merely claim a token. Use illustrated exhibit artifacts and a clean passport page as recurring creative motifs. Sell campaign packages to venues and optional commemorative collectibles, while admitting that stamps do not imply investment value.

### 5. Stadium Sidequest

Give adult event attendees a venue-approved route through merchandise, concession and fan-zone checkpoints. Promote match-day challenges on X with teams or actual venue operators confirming participation. Use ticket-shaped badges, a compact venue map and real redemption clips; separate the reward from a game's outcome. Monetize organizer campaigns and disclose the exact funded prize budget and eligibility window.

### 6. Bookstore Hunt

Attach short literary clues to participating bookstores, with a bookseller verifying completion and awarding a reading-club collectible or discount. Promote one clue at a time on X and let readers share solved routes and book recommendations. Use collectible book-spine art over real shopfronts, followed by authentic bookseller videos. Charge for sponsored routes or club memberships without requiring the purchase of a speculative asset to join.

### 7. Clean Street

Reward verified participation in organized cleanup sessions, with the local organizer confirming attendance and completion. Use X to announce bounded events and publish organizer-approved before/after evidence plus public reward accounting. Keep a recognizable glove or leaf mascot and include real volunteers, avoiding unsupported claims that a photograph alone proves impact. Municipal or local business sponsorship funds rewards and a disclosed coordination fee.

### 8. City Food Tour

Offer a rotating set of restaurant checkpoints where counter verification unlocks tasting credits and a completion collectible. Promote themed weekly routes on X, pairing short food videos with clear availability and limits. Use one consistent tasting-ticket object and real meal photographs rather than generic financial imagery. Charge participating venues for completed campaign visits and measure whether visitors make worthwhile incremental purchases.

### 9. Campus Quest

Pilot an adult-only campus route covering bookstores, cafés and public events, with organizers verifying attendance. Promote club-versus-club participation challenges on X with opt-in teams and genuine activity clips. Use a collectible campus pennant and simple route graphics that identify approved public locations. Earn through organizer or retailer campaigns, with fixed budgets and minimal retention of participant location history.

### 10. Commuter Collect

Let station retailers fund a small daily collectible or discount for a verified visit during an ordinary commute. Promote one station pilot on X with a short off-platform walk to the shop and a real redemption, then expand based on repeat participation. Use collectible transit-ticket artwork, station-specific maps and a public remaining-reward counter. Monetize retailer campaigns while keeping every checkpoint away from tracks, restricted areas and crowd bottlenecks.
