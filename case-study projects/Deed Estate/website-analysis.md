# Deed Estate — website analysis

Research date: **27 September 2026, Europe/Moscow**. Primary website: [deed.estate](https://deed.estate/). Historical design evidence comes from the supplied contract-search images, with enlarged crops preserved in [research-evidence/crops](research-evidence/crops/).

## 1. Essence, product and narrative

DEED presented a real-estate rental-income vault: access to a portfolio of apartments without handling tenants, maintenance or a mortgage. The historical homepage headline was “Rental property, without the property.” and the account bio framed the benefit as “Rent is due. Shouldn’t you get a cut?” The lower homepage heading, “The money comes from tenants.”, attempted to connect a crypto share to a familiar external source of cash flow. Sources: the homepage screenshots in **M131, M166 and M169** of [x-mentions-flow.md](x-mentions-flow.md), and the project’s [X-Flow.md](X-Flow.md).

The website was presented as both an acquisition landing page and a wallet-connected app. Its campaign described an apartment portfolio, net rent retained inside a vault, tradable shares and monthly apartment-level reporting called **The Roll**. The economic story was accumulation of net rent in the value of shares, rather than simply a monthly cheque to every token holder; the captured project posts support retained rent, but do not establish the legal entitlement or deployed accounting mechanism.

The phrase “ETF” was a marketing comparison. No fund registration, property-title documentation, custodian agreement or binding token-holder rights were recovered. Similarly, the account’s references to Robinhood describe its positioning and claimed chain deployment; they do not prove Robinhood Markets operated or endorsed the project.

## 2. Technical functionality — observed controls and claimed behaviour

### What was visible in historical screenshots

- **Homepage navigation:** DEED logo at upper left, a compact rounded navigation group near the top centre with **Portfolio, The Roll, Dashboard, FAQ, Docs**, and a white **Connect wallet** control at upper right.
- **Primary conversion controls:** a lime **Connect wallet** button under the headline, with an outlined **See how it works** button beside it.
- **Portfolio/map page:** a large US map with state outlines and yellow-green location markers; a horizontal row of cards showing **62 Apartments, 5 Buildings, 5 Cities, 92% Occupied, $78,656 Rent collected last month**. These are displayed historical project claims. Several markers are visible in the southeast, but the screenshot does not establish all five locations or property addresses. See [enlarged historical property screenshot](research-evidence/crops/historical-properties.png), from M045.
- **A report/table view:** a pale page appears as the second image in Jeff’s paid-partnership post M062. Its complete rows and controls are too small and clipped to transcribe reliably; the image is evidence of a report-shaped interface, not a verified rent-roll dataset.
- **Landing-page sections:** the lower start of “The money comes from tenants.” is visible. The rest of the homepage below the captured viewport is unavailable.

### What the project advertised

The account said its property map tracker was live, that holders shared exposure to an apartment vault, and that rent after expenses remained in the vault each month. It promised publication of The Roll on the first of each month with actual payments, late rent and vacant units, and a public mirror of the account additionally described a redemption queue and checking rent against bank records; the mirror’s additional claims are summarized in [other-info.md](other-info.md), separately from screenshot transcription.

Promotional posts mentioned **100 USDG** deposits and obtaining shares. However, promotional descriptions used both **DEED** and **vDEED**, and the verified target address was also listed as a fixed-supply launchpad token. No working vault address or onchain connection between the target DEED token and an apartment-income vault was established. The project’s rent-roll publication date was still a future promise relative to its September 18–22 launch campaign; no actual October report was supplied.

The presence of a wallet button does not prove deposit, withdrawal or portfolio functionality worked. A map and counters do not prove ownership of the mapped properties. No deposit, approval, swap or redemption was performed during this research.

### What works at the research date

The homepage and the project-advertised [properties route](https://deed.estate/properties) were both visited in a browser. Each displayed a dark Vercel error page with **“This page doesn’t exist”**, **404**, and **DEPLOYMENT_NOT_FOUND**, plus **Go back**, documentation and debugging controls; the web retrieval tool independently returned 404 for both URLs. There was therefore no accessible project navigation to explore or app flow to test.

A dated Wayback recovery attempt could not be accessed through the research tool. Consequently, **wallet compatibility, deposits, share pricing, redemption queues, filters, map click behaviour, dashboards, FAQ answers, documentation, report downloads, accessibility and mobile responsiveness remain unverified**. The domain’s failure identifies an unavailable deployment at this observation, not who removed it or why.

## 3. Design and branding

**Palette:** the historical app used deep forest/emerald green as its dominant canvas, warm cream for major text and the logo, muted green for secondary panels, and yellow-lime for calls to action and map markers. It resembled an orderly property-investment product. The current black 404 page is hosting-provider error styling, not the original DEED design.

**Typography:** headings were large, bold sans-serif with tight spacing and short stacked lines; body copy was much smaller and arranged in compact readable-width blocks. Exact font family, numerical sizes and CSS colours cannot be established from screenshots. The homepage’s central headline supplied the hierarchy; the property page switched to a left-aligned title above data cards and map.

**Imagery and symbols:** the logo is a cream stack of roof/floor chevrons forming a compact high-rise silhouette. It appeared in the account avatar, website header, cream/green apartment posters, market-page previews and sponsored posts. The banner and link preview used cream towers converging around green sky; other creatives used miniature apartment buildings or cinematic night-time apartment footage with warmly lit windows. These make “apartments generate rent” understandable before reading the longer product text.

**Layout and components:** the homepage centred its proposition within generous green space and thin concentric outline rings; two small pill-like buttons sat directly below. Navigation was shallow and compact. The portfolio page made the first card cream and the remaining cards darker green, prioritizing apartment count without giving every number equal weight. The wide map made the portfolio appear tangible and geographically distributed.

**Motion:** project posts contain video previews, but a static screenshot cannot establish website animation, hover effects, scroll transitions or whether the rings moved. No animation is claimed as observed.

**Brand consistency and marketing contribution:** the same green/cream architecture, heavy cream headings and building mark connected the site to the account’s posters and to third-party paid-partnership screenshots. Reusing the homepage or logo gave external posts instant visual continuity. The vacancy poster’s one dark window translated a reporting feature into a recognizable creative: an empty apartment remained in the report. These are plausible contributors to recognition and shareability, not measured effects on conversion.

## 4. Assessment

The acquisition design succeeded at expressing one promise quickly: a share of rental economics without landlord work. Its map, occupancy cards and report vocabulary strengthened the impression of a concrete product. The supplied evidence establishes the appearance and messaging, while the unavailable website and missing vault/property records prevent an assessment of delivery; [project-overview.md](project-overview.md) incorporates this distinction into the case study.
